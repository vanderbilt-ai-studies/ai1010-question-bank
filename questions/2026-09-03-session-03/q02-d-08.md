**Question:** The lecture argued that a small model on very fast hardware might match a much larger model on some work, then said the full case could not be made yet. What was the argument, and what was missing?

- A. A small model carries the same world knowledge as a large one; the case waits until training is covered.
- B. Speed makes each individual token more accurate; the case waits until hardware is covered in depth.
- C. Cheap speed lets a small model work at length; the full case needs agents and the harness.
- D. Open-weight models are consistently larger than hosted ones; the case waits on the licensing question.

<details>
<summary>Show the answer and why each option is right or wrong</summary>

**Answer: C**

- A. Not quite. World knowledge is exactly what a smaller model gives up -- that is the trade you make when you go smaller. The argument for small models has to come from somewhere else than pretending the gap is not there.
- B. Not quite. Running faster does not change what any single token is -- one token is one token either way. The gain from speed is that a great deal of work becomes cheap in time, not that the work becomes better per token.
- C. Correct. If working through a problem costs almost nothing in time, a small model can afford a great deal of it, and on some tasks that closes the gap to a very large model. The reason the argument is incomplete is that what a model can do with all that cheap work depends on the agent and its harness, which come later in the course.
- D. Not quite. Openly available models are generally in the small-to-medium range, which is the reason fast chips suit them. Licensing was not the gap in the argument -- the missing piece was agents.
