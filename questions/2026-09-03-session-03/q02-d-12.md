**Question:** If nobody writes grammar into a language model, where does whatever it represents about the rules of language come from?

- A. From a rule table that the architecture supplies before training starts.
- B. From your prompt, which has to carry the rules the model needs each time.
- C. From the training signal itself, by guessing what comes next.
- D. From a grammar checker applied to the model's output after it is generated.

<details>
<summary>Show the answer and why each option is right or wrong</summary>

**Answer: C**

- A. Not quite. The architecture supplies a capability -- a shape that can learn -- not any content. There is no table of rules anywhere in it before training runs.
- B. Not quite. Your prompt supplies the context for one exchange, not the model's grasp of language. It arrives at the model already able to handle grammar, which is why a two-word prompt still gets a fluent reply.
- C. Correct. The instruction during training is only ever predict the next thing in this signal, and being corrected on that is the sole source of what the model represents. It is a strong claim and worth sitting with: the structure was never told to it.
- D. Not quite. There is no separate corrector downstream of generation -- the output is simply the tokens the model chose. Fluency comes from the weights, which is to say from training.
